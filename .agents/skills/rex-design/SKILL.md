# REX Design

Choose the right job for the prompt before designing. Visual mode makes a distinctive, high-quality visual experience; Production mode makes a dependable task interface. Neither mode permits invented claims, inaccessible controls, or a static render mislabeled as live behavior. Start from the existing product and code, not a generic visual trend.

## Route the prompt: Visual or Production

Read the full prompt and identify its *intended outcome*, not just a keyword. Choose one mode before writing code and state the reason in the review record. Explicit audience, functional constraints, and real product requirements override a generic request for polish. If the brief names a launch, campaign, portfolio, editorial story, showcase, striking hero, art direction, cinematic motion, or a visually ambitious concept, choose **Visual** even when it also says "page" or "dashboard". If the brief asks people to complete a real workflow, inspect data, configure a product, recover from errors, or use a page in production, choose **Production** even if it asks to look good. A request can be ambitious because of its business scope or number of features without being visually ambitious: do not route it to Visual for size alone. When both are present, preserve hard functional requirements, then use Visual only if visual impact is explicitly the primary success criterion; otherwise use Production. If neither is clear, default to Production and record the assumption. Ask only if a wrong choice changes the purpose or audience and the prompt cannot resolve it.

Visual is not a shortcut around production basics, and Production is not a ban on original art direction. Mode changes where design effort and review attention concentrate, not whether accessibility and honest state behavior matter. Do not call a mode a quality rating.

## Landing-page craft from observed pages

The reference study is a range of 101 distinct company-produced sites across software, hardware, retail, culture, publishing, public service and finance, inspected in desktop and phone views with recorded loading/overlay limits. These are observed pages, not proof of the designers' identities or of conversion performance. Use the original brief and the product's own material, not any site's layout, campaign asset, brand color, headline or animation. The study's source ledger stays private; examples below are evidence of a *mechanism*, not templates to implement.

First choose what the person came to do. A transaction can put the working task first (Wise's converter, Delta's flight search); an editorial site can put one specific story and its provenance first (NPR, The Guardian); a physical product can put the real object and its material behavior first (Converse, Leica, Tiffany); a complex service can offer a question and distinct routes (IBM, Dell). Huly, Tesla and Codex are different answers to different jobs. Do not require an input/output widget, cinematic photo, logo wall or two buttons of every page. If real assets or product behavior are unavailable, say what is illustrative and make an honest authored example rather than a fabricated product screenshot. A static composition is valid when interaction has no job.

Before committing, write a compact **content and evidence map**: audience and first decision; the specific object, story or task that can carry the first view; source and status of each claim and visual; the next action that actually exists; and what the next chapter adds. Extract the brief's promised inputs and outcome separately. An editorial or object-led hero is free to avoid a widget, but it cannot quietly replace a requested choice-and-result product with a mood image or slogan. If the brief asks for three independent choices, preserve three legible choices and show how each affects the result; a preselected example or pagination through presets is not equivalent. Consider at least two *different organizing ideas* (for example, object-led versus task-led), not two skins of the same hero. Choose by the brief's evidence, audience and delivery frame. If neither has enough real material, seek it or narrow the claim. In an established product, inspect its existing assets, voice and flows before replacing them.

First pass the brief's **result contract** before any taste or ownable-proof test. List each promised input, the source-backed or openly authored rule, and the concrete deliverable or decision a person can actually use. Show a legible example of that deliverable and a second divergent state; an input echo, mood line, decorative symbol, unexplained timer or generic status is not a result. If the promised deliverable cannot be sourced, make its limits explicit and use a truthful worked example or stop the concept; never invent a prescription or live operation. Only after that contract passes should art direction compete for space. Before styling, run an **ownable proof test** on the proposed hero. Name one real or explicitly illustrative material, object or work record from this product; the visual change it can truthfully show; and the viewer's useful inference. Draw two different layouts using that same proof at delivery size. Cover the brand and delete the headline: if the hero is still just a familiar split, cards, pill controls or a generic progress dial that could sell an unrelated product, keep the content contract and redesign the visual thesis, not merely the colors. A photographed material, a physical cause-and-effect scene, a legible work record or a distinct editorial treatment may carry the idea; no one form is required. A signature visual for a choice-and-result product must carry the useful result data itself, not merely surround it: a movement sequence with recognizable moves, an order discrepancy with both available decision paths, or a lamp/shade with a clearly changed light footprint on the same desk. Test it with headlines hidden. If the visual only says "this is our brand" while the promised result is pushed into small copy or offscreen, remove or redesign the visual. For a state change, hold unaffected parts of the scene steady and make the actual effect visible at the object and its receiver, not only in a label or button. Do not depict a fictional product as a real photograph or fabricate an unverified capability to win the taste test.

Make the first view a deliberate editorial cut, not a prescribed anatomy. A strong product photograph can be proof of form and material without exposing the product's algorithm. A real UI may be proof when it is legible and representative. A terse story with an immediately visible evidence image can establish an editorial premise. The title, object and route should work together, but the relative weight depends on the job. Ask what a viewer can recognize in one scan, and whether the copy adds a specific fact rather than explaining a generic graphic. Restraint, typography, crop and negative space are decisions with opportunity costs, not a palette to apply. Avoid proxy excitement from gradients, floating blobs, pills, fake dashboards and meaningless motion.

**Recompose for phone.** Check a short 390×650 frame with real header/host height and a settled full-size view. Preserve the subject, readable claim and honest route by changing sequence, crop and density, not merely scaling desktop. Tiffany changes its photographic crop and text order; Kickstarter stacks discovery and the named project; IBM removes the secondary news rail. A first-view crop may show only a purposeful glimpse of the next chapter, but must not conceal the primary mechanism or control with its legible effect, the only meaningful action, or a limitation essential to the claim. For a brief whose point is changing the result, place the primary control and its effect together inside the actual host-framed 390×650 opening; a heading or the start of a result at the bottom edge does not count. If the subject or effect is clipped, reframe or change the sequence; do not rescue the crop by shrinking labels, controls or the subject below useful size. When several facts cannot fit legibly together, provide a readable summary and an obvious route to full detail instead of compressing an entire desktop chain into miniature phone type. Test an overlay, slow media and no-media state: many surveyed pages lost their phone hierarchy to cookie/signup dialogs or lazy assets. Do not mistake those failures for craft.

Give subsequent sections distinct evidence jobs. A hero can set an object or premise; the next chapter may show use, choice, mechanism, inventory, origin, a real example, an objection or a limitation. Converse shifts from model portrait to weather detail; Kickstarter names a creator and project state; Canon moves from camera to deals and learning. If later cards simply restate the first promise, cut them. Use motion only when it changes understanding, guides attention or shows a true state change; inspect resting, moving, reduced-motion and failure states when motion is used. A page can be quietly static and excellent.

When a brief includes a human decision, show the actual distinct choices as visible, honest, comparable controls; a sentence saying "approve or reject" is not two paths. Do not style one as a real enabled action while the other is merely decorative or disabled unless the product state explains why. If the experience is only an illustrative preview, label the controls and their outcome as examples rather than live writes. Give both permitted decisions comparable visual weight and legible keyboard-reachable labels; a solid primary action against a faint outline can bias a safety review even when both controls technically exist. Show the exact record identifier, the source and proposed values **with units**, and the direction of the proposed change in one sentence beside the decision (for example, "Change order 418 from 12 units to 10 units"). Use the actual source's vocabulary; do not turn a warehouse count into an unsupported shelf claim. Make provenance visible in the composition when it determines trust, rather than hiding it in a tiny caption below the numbers. If a later section walks through the same example, it must reveal new evidence, an edge case or a real next step, not repeat the hero at larger size. At short-phone size, keep the illustrative-status label and what each human choice means readable in the same visible decision area; if they fall below the fold, recompose instead of shrinking captions.

Before handing off, inspect settled desktop, short phone and a later chapter as pixels. Include the second settled state in the handoff for a page that claims adaptation; if only the default state was captured, report the mechanism unverified rather than inferring it from code or controls. Hide the brand name and ask whether the content, crop, type rhythm and action still tell *this* product's story. Inspect the same widths against the prior build, separately scoring taste, task clarity and phone integrity; a clearer mechanism can still look worse. Record one substantive weakness and its correction, plus any missing assets or states. Do not report compliance with a checklist as evidence of human-level quality.

## Pre-build concept gate

Before choosing a layout or writing UI code, compare at least **two structurally different concepts** from the frozen brief, adding more only when uncertainty warrants them, not surface treatments of the same interaction. For each, write the core user-visible claim, the subject-specific rule or content that makes it true, the starting state, the meaningful action or progression if any, and the resulting payoff. Compare the concepts without styling: remove type, color, motion, and explanatory labels, then describe what a person would learn, do, or see change. If the payoff is only a generic symbol, a repeated line, or an invented fact presented as a discovery, reject it before implementation. Do not add interactivity when a static page serves the brief better.

Make that comparison visible before polishing: assemble a one-screen, side-by-side first-view study of the two strongest **structurally different** ideas, using the same honest product evidence and real words. Include both desktop and short-phone crops at delivery size, not two descriptions or the same card layout in different colors. Hide the brand and headline. Point to the useful relation, object or consequence a viewer can still read in each crop, then choose one and record the specific reason the other lost. A missing product asset is a reason to narrow the concept, not to dress a generic card as evidence. This study is a judgment aid, not an automatic taste gate; if neither reads, revise before writing the polished page.

For a candidate that promises to reveal a relation, apply a **relationship-proof baseline** before layout or visual polish. Name the exact source-backed relation that its spatial or temporal behavior makes legible, and put the real words and marks at their intended relative positions in a stripped desktop and 390×650 sketch. Compare it in one scan against a well-typeset plain list, diff, map or node-link view of the same source. Can the viewer notice a task-relevant relation or consequence faster and more accurately, without reading a headline or legend to compensate? If not, reject the candidate before code, even when it is clear and honest. A container around a graph, a decorated diff or an editorial headline is not a new relationship. Keep every relation source-traceable and unknowns visible; this gate does not permit invented data or an inferred edge styled as fact. Record the baseline and rejection reason in the review.

Apply a **claim/content contract test** to every finalist. Quote the exact source fact or state that entails each reveal and show how the reveal changes the reading of another part of the content or the user's next decision. Distinguish interpretation from fact, and authored fiction from discovery; label fiction honestly if the brief permits it. If a concept depends on missing content, request or research that content rather than designing around a placeholder. For Production, test the proposed outcome against real data, permissions, and service states rather than a plausible local mock. Keep the candidate ideas and rejection reasons in a short review record, choose a survivor for its actual payoff, and stop to revise the concept or brief when none survive. This is a pre-build decision, not a promise that the finished pixels or behavior will pass review.

Before a data-led concept survives, sketch its **delivery-size proof** alongside the no-visuals test: at the actual first viewport on desktop and a short mobile screen, identify the focal mark, its minimum readable size, the needed legend/source/uncertainty, and what must be visible together. Do not confuse verified data provenance with a compelling visual idea. A fine-line map that only reads after zoom or a citation that falls below the fold fails the concept at that size, even when its facts and geometry are correct. Recompose the idea or choose a different medium rather than squeezing source notes into tiny type. Verify these projected constraints in rendered pixels after implementation; a sketch cannot certify readability.

### First-view ownership gate

At the actual desktop first view and 390×650 short-mobile view, the brief's subject, evidence or interaction must be immediately legible. A headline may carry the premise when the words are the content, but it should not push the only proof or next route out of reach. Remove explanatory copy temporarily: can someone still locate the focal evidence, its state and the next meaningful action? Reject the composition if a headline or explainer owns the first view while a small widget, map, receipt or interaction carries the real work. Do not fix this by deleting necessary provenance, uncertainty or recovery; enlarge and recompose the evidence so those remain readable together. Compare the settled pixels in both viewports, including the surrounding host, before approving. For a claim that depends on two or more related states, marks or receipts, sketch and inspect all endpoints plus their join inside the *visible* host-framed first viewport. Do not count an endpoint below the fold, behind a tab, or readable only in a full-page capture as a one-scan pass; compare the same cropped frame with the plain baseline before implementation and again after rendering. If the relation cannot fit at legible size, simplify the composition or stop the concept rather than hiding an endpoint.

### Ordinary product landing-page specificity gate

A brief such as "make a landing page for an app" names a category, not a brand system or a product screenshot. Before choosing a hero composition, state the intended person, their first decision, the one defensible product promise, and which facts/assets are *not* supplied. If the product is fictional, label it; write illustrative UI and claims as examples, not as live metrics, store availability or measured outcomes. Do not fill an empty brief by stacking the familiar anatomy: pill-shaped category eyebrow, one differently colored headline word, two equal hero CTAs, a generic phone silhouette, invented streak/readiness numbers, and three interchangeable feature cards. These are symptoms, not prohibited components. Keep a component only if its content and placement make a specific decision clearer than the plain version.

For each pre-build candidate, put the **product's own evidence** in the first host-framed viewport: this may be an actual object, a representative use scene, a live task, a real product interface, or an honest worked example. Show an input, decision or consequence when the brief promises one; do not impose a compulsory widget on an object-, story- or catalog-led page. When a product claims to generate an outcome from inputs, identify the exact input dimensions, the mapping (verified or explicitly authored) and the usable result before choosing the visual form. Keep the input labels, selected values, result and reason findable together without hiding required dimensions behind a carousel, faux selection or explanatory section. A product's promise is not fulfilled by restating a selected value in larger type. Sketch the actual 390×650 crop with navigation and any host header *before* choosing type, asset or animation. Name what the user could infer if all marketing copy were hidden. If the purported proof lives only below the phone fold or the hero art is an empty prop, reject or recompose the candidate. A photograph can be stronger than a fabricated interface; a real product screenshot can be stronger than a photograph. Do not invent an interface and present it as real merely to satisfy a visual-asset quota.

Run a **template-substitution test** before polishing: swap the brand and category words with an unrelated product. If the focal visual, section order, and meaning of the primary action still work unchanged, the page has not earned its design. Replace the generic visual with a truthful product-specific example, change the section's job, or state that the brief lacks the content for a convincing product demo. Test with the rendered desktop and short-mobile opening, not just a written rationale. Choose the typeface and hierarchy for the audience and subject; a system font is acceptable when it fits, but using the browser default because no typographic decision was made is not art direction. Avoid downloading a font when licensing or network access is unclear; keep a tested fallback. One honest route to the next action beats a pair of decorative CTAs; if the product cannot be downloaded, do not label a dead button "Download".

At handoff, compare the new page against a frozen generic baseline at the same widths. List the exact template symptoms removed, the subject-specific evidence visible in the first viewport, the remaining generic areas and any unequal delivery conditions. A host's checklist pass or REX's six capture slots does not establish taste. If the first view still reads as a logo/headline/empty phone/CTA arrangement, call it a failed study rather than a design win.

### Short-phone payoff and audience register

A mobile first view does not pass because a card header or the top of an output is visible. Before implementation, draw the actual 390×650 viewport with navigation and host chrome at their real heights. Outline the **payoff bundle**: each required input dimension and its selected value when the brief promises a choice, a legible usable result with its reason or evidence, and an honest next action or limitation. If there are no required inputs, use the actual subject, evidence and route instead. Where all supporting detail cannot fit, keep a meaningful result summary and the mechanism/effect legible in the opening, with an explicit route to fuller detail below; do not shrink essential text or controls to manufacture a fit. A phone view that shows an option label but clips the consequence or hides another required control fails. Mark each bundle element's bottom edge. If the result title fits but its reason, useful details or next route are clipped, the first view fails. Recompose the page around that bundle: shorten the header and setup, reduce the number of choices, remove repeated explanation, or move secondary detail below the fold while preserving the essential reason. Never solve it by shrinking type or touch targets. After rendering, inspect the same cropped, host-framed first view in at least two divergent input states and after reversal, not a full-page screenshot. A result that only becomes understandable after scrolling is a mobile regression even if its desktop taste improves.

Write a one-sentence **audience register** before choosing display type, casing, saturation, imagery or motion: who is reading, what they may fear or hope, and what the page should sound like to them. Compare two materially different first-view treatments in grayscale, with the exact same words, for fit with that person. Subject material is only a lead, not a mandate: a workout product for beginners need not use the visual language of an aggressive gym poster. Check the final copy as well as its styling. A loud, condensed all-caps headline or an extreme name can make gentle onboarding feel like a dare. Keep intensity only when the brief, audience and actual product voice support it.

When showing an illustrative result in a safety- or suitability-sensitive area such as exercise, distinguish the known product behavior from a made-up prescription. Exact reps, rest intervals, diagnoses or personal recommendations require supplied rules and an appropriate scope. If those are absent, show an explicitly authored example or the choice-to-outcome structure without implying that the page knows what is right for this person. Place the example/limitation label beside the result, not only in a footer. Later sections must add evidence or answer an objection rather than repeat the first-view explanation.

### Causal visual and result handoff

A signature visual device must explain the product's decision, not merely make the input look memorable. Before adding an orbit, ring, progress arc, connecting line or animated mark, write an **input-to-mark ledger**: what known or openly authored rule maps each input to the mark, what part of the mark changes for two divergent choices, and what a viewer can infer from that change without reading a headline. If the same visual survives an input change with only a number or accent swapped, treat it as decoration. Either give it a truthful causal job (for example, a segment map where the allocation actually changes) or remove it. Do not assign an invented scientific rationale to a fictional rule to rescue the device. Test the first and changed states side by side at desktop and 390×650, then reverse the choice; the source and result must stay in agreement. **Deliver that comparison in the review evidence**, not only in the implementation: record the exact input values, the two settled frames at the same crop, and the visible part of the result that differs. A six-slot capture set may contain duplicates, and a scroll film of one initial state does not show that the promised mechanism adapts. If the page is static or the brief has no truthful second state, say so rather than staging an invented change.

When the page claims a choice produces a result, its first-view sentence must name a **concrete consequence** of the choice, not a mood slogan, selected-option echo or category label. State what changed in the authored example or verified product behavior and why, at the level the source supports. Test two divergent combinations of all promised inputs: can someone compare the actual deliverable (for example, different exercise examples, a corrected order line, or a changed light direction) without relying on color, slogans or a numerical badge alone? If the source lacks a rule for a real personalized output, label a useful worked example beside its result and keep the promised controls honest rather than implying a functioning recommendation engine. Do not invent reps, rest, health suitability or personalization when the brief supplies no decision rule. Next to that result, make the route legible in words: "Explore the example" is a route to more illustrative detail, while a bare diagonal arrow is not a next step. Do not offer download, enrollment or an operating recommendation the page cannot fulfill. If the user has to scroll to discover what the result means, revisit the short-phone payoff bundle rather than adding a detached explainer.

For every below-fold section, write the new question it answers. When the first view shows a choice or adjustable object, prefer a second *divergent, source-backed state* with the changed consequence in the next chapter over a larger repeat of the hero. If the next chapter cannot add that proof, use a new limitation, source or objection instead; do not invent a second output just to fill a section. Hide its heading and compare its content with the first-view choice/result. If it repeats the same inputs, promise or explanation, cut it; do not count a different layout as new evidence. A shorter honest page is better than a long sequence of renamed summaries.

### Distinctive language beyond a clear first view

A product-specific input/output in the hero can still be a generic quiz in a new color. For a thin consumer brief, compare plausible evidence forms before selecting a candidate: an authored editorial or photographic story, an honest worked example, and an interaction only when a real response rule is available. Some briefs have just one truthful form. A mock recommendation is not an app. If using an illustrative UI, place its "example, not the app" status next to the exact result and carry that status through the next section; do not make a polished simulated interface imply working personalization. For each choice a visitor can change, write down the source-backed or openly authored mapping to its result, try divergent inputs and reverse them. If every path yields the same generic encouragement or cosmetic change, remove the control and show a truthful static example. A headline that could be printed on any wellness, finance or productivity site is not a product consequence.

Before choosing accent colors, cards or decorative marks, make a **visual-language sheet** with the subject's physical or conceptual material, a governing shape or rhythm, a typographic contrast and one color relationship. Show where each appears in the hero and at least one later section, and why each belongs to this product rather than to a current web trend. Compare at least two materially different directions in grayscale and at actual first-view sizes; only then add color. Reject a loud stripe, lime result block, pale-tint section or default display font if it merely supplies excitement to an otherwise conventional card. This is not a required palette, font or shape: a quiet neutral system may be best, and a vivid palette can work when it earns a subject-specific job. Verify contrast, licensing, fallback and mobile size in pixels.

Give each section a **different job** in a short page map: prove one claim, show a consequence or limitation the hero could not, answer an objection, or provide an honest next step. Hide the headings and compare the actual content. If a later section restates the hero's inputs as steps or wraps the same output in another card, cut it or bring genuinely new evidence. Do not stretch a one-view idea into repeated marketing sections just to look like a full landing page. For an embedded phone view, reserve host-header height and ensure the mechanism, key outcome and next safe route remain legible together; if a dense questionnaire squeezes options to small touch targets, use fewer choices, progressive disclosure or a different evidence form instead of shrinking type.

Run a **taste adversary** on the settled desktop/mobile captures and one below-fold crop. Compare the selected first view to a plain, well-typeset data-first composition carrying the same facts and actions: does its signature visual make a product-specific relation easier to see or make the subject more recognizable without relying on a brand label? If it only restyles the same cards or delays the useful evidence, remove the device rather than decorate it further. Ask: can a different brand inherit the colors, card layout, type and section rhythm unchanged; does the imagined product feel more real than the evidence warrants; does the product-specific object or outcome command attention without an oversized slogan; is the final state more distinctive than the initial one? Name at least one failure and make one substantive correction before asking an independent critic to compare against the frozen prior render. If the correction spends a study's revision cap, stop rather than silently reworking it. REX capture acceptance, clearer first-view comprehension and a higher taste score are separate outcomes; report each from its own evidence.

## Visual mode: an art-directed experience

Begin with a one-sentence visual thesis tied to the subject: what the viewer sees immediately, what changes if anything, and why that form belongs to the story. Sketch two structural compositions with different narrative rhythms; pick one for its message rather than fashion. Plan a visual system across the *whole* page: type scale and contrast, authored imagery or source-licensed assets where available, crop at desktop and mobile, color and texture, section transitions, and motion only when it has a purpose. Visual mode aims for an authored experience; it need not animate when stillness serves the subject better. Choose the motion mechanism from the page's story: entrance, state change, cursor or gesture, timeline, or scroll. A continuous visual control should map its reported value to a visible progress state, work in reverse and from the keyboard, and update immediately rather than becoming inert when motion is reduced. For a multi-parameter Visual scene, make each control change a distinct visible property and keep the combined state readable in text. Exercise each axis separately, their joint extremes and reversal; make the current values accessible without depending only on color or motion. A decorative multi-slider that leaves the art essentially unchanged is not a meaningful interaction. A two-state physical metaphor can use a reversible local control rather than scroll pinning: verify opening, midpoint, final resting state, reverse path and keyboard activation, and keep any toggle label and pressed state aligned with the rendered art. CSS motion is acceptable when it genuinely expresses the concept; a library or a canvas is not a quality credential. When scroll-driven choreography actually explains the narrative, use GSAP with ScrollTrigger and design the pinning, scrub, and scene transitions around the content. Do not add a stock GSAP scroll sequence to every Visual page; repeated reveal recipes feel like a template. Record why GSAP scroll is used or why another motion mechanism fits better. If the idea needs 3D, use geometry, lighting, occlusion and camera movement that make depth legible; a flat disc with 3D-sounding copy is not a substitute. Inspect changed pixels from at least two camera positions and an actual motion interval, not just the presence of Three.js or a WebGL canvas. Keep a readable static fallback and test a rendered reduced-motion state. Exercise both initial asset/renderer failure and a later graphics-context loss or equivalent runtime failure; do not leave active controls over an inert canvas or call an unloaded scene complete. For ambient motion that runs longer than a brief transition, give the viewer a visible pause/resume control without hiding meaning. The paused image should remain a valid composition, and the control label and pressed state must agree with actual computed animation state. Respect reduced motion at initialization and after a preference change; do not restart against the viewer’s choice when a tab regains focus. Hide a script-dependent pause control until initialization succeeds, leaving a readable static scene without JS. Test moving frames, paused frames separated by time, resume, keyboard activation, narrow layout and reduced-motion pixels. Avoid motion that hides information or blocks controls; keep the final scene coherent at any scroll position. Evaluate the first viewport in the actual delivery frame, not only the page iframe or isolated local tab. A host header, safe area or embedded card can consume much of a phone screen; inspect how much of the central visual behavior and primary route remain visible at 390px and the intended device height. If the artwork appears only after scrolling, rebalance vertical spacing, copy and artwork placement rather than calling a cropped heading a complete first impression. Keep the page readable when the host shell changes size; do not solve the crop by shrinking text below a usable size. The first viewport should communicate the idea without an explanation paragraph, and later sections should sustain it rather than reverting to generic cards. Use an original asset when imagery is central and a suitable tool is available; record origin, license/ownership, size, performance cost, and graceful fallback. Do not fake brands, reviews, sales numbers or product screenshots. No random decoration or borrowed layout in place of a concept.

## Failure classes to test before polishing

Treat each critique as a transferable failure class rather than adding a rule for one object or scene. Record the claim, evidence, counterexample and next test in a review ledger. These checks are not a pass certificate; select the ones relevant to the brief and test their actual rendered and interactive states.

- **Verdict strength and negative geometry:** match the final status wording to the strongest claim the source actually establishes: "no path" is different from "not verified," and an unknown status is different from a confirmed absence. In a route, dependency or connectivity diagram, do not draw a dashed or red connector through a segment that does not exist; color and dash alone still read as a connection. End the known path at the last reached node, isolate the unreachable target with space, and put the source and freshness caveat beside that break. Test the drawing with its labels hidden, then restore accessible text and check the source-specific wording at delivery size. A correct data label does not rescue a visually false relationship.
- **Evidence drift:** a source assertion, control label, accessibility text, receipt or animation frame cannot prove a settled visual or service state. Compare the exact claim to the same-crop pixels or service receipt at the claimed time, size and delivery frame. Mark untested claims unknown.
- **Coordinate and causal mismatch:** related marks, objects and effects need one declared coordinate system, transform, source and ordering rule. Derive dependent geometry from shared parameters; test boundaries and intermediate/reversed states. A mask or explanatory disclaimer cannot repair contradictory relationships. Use a defensible model if claiming physical behavior; otherwise describe the abstraction honestly.
- **Responsive geometry drift:** preserve intended shape proportions and relationships across aspect ratios and the host frame. Recompute dependent geometry together, not just the focal SVG or image. Check narrow and wide pixels for distortion, overlaps, clearance and recovery.
- **Control-semantic drift:** write the invariant, zero state and end state before styling. Exercise start, intermediates, end, reverse, keyboard and reduced motion. Verify the visible effect matches the value and label, not merely the event handler.
- **Scene identity under selection:** when a control claims to change one physical material or object, keep the surrounding scene stable: hands, tools, light and other materials must not change with it. A whole-frame color grade is a mood choice, not evidence of selecting cloth or another material. Compare identical crops at each state and name the effect truthfully if the scene cannot preserve that boundary.
- **Promised consequence:** a state change that only recolors or renames the same asset is not a new visible outcome. Hide the labels and compare settled frames to a plain baseline: what subject-specific consequence can the viewer see? The headline must also have an observable referent in the page; if the page promises a person-centered result but only shows a generic object, show the person-specific consequence or narrow the claim.
- **Idea substitution:** a decorative label, reveal, frame, generic layout or animation is not a second consequence. Ask what changes because of the central rule and whether a viewer could see that result without an explanation. Compare live references for mechanisms and fit, not their surface grammar.
- **Plausibility and state integrity:** inspect impossible intersections, detached shadows, occlusion, missing data, stale reviews and optimistic statuses according to the page's own premise. State which facts are modeled, simulated, observed or confirmed by a service.
- **Transformation custody:** if a before/after promises a new arrangement, conserve the original entities and any quantities the story depends on. Give them one coordinate system, consistent units, visible paths or reconstructible positions, and an honest account of what moved, remained fixed, became constrained, or could not move. A disappearance is not a mechanism. If the benefit is space or absence, show the opening and its boundaries rather than filling it with a new decorative object. Hide the headline and explanatory label while judging whether the visual still proves its central claim. A clean but idealized animation must not imply that every real constraint is movable.
- **Material-vocabulary break:** enumerate the visible primitives in the before and after states. If the result needs a solid stroke, silhouette, glyph, shadow or object not built from the conserved starting pieces, reject the claimed transformation or explicitly disclose the added element. Compare the rendered pixels, not only counts in code.
- **Stock-icon payoff:** hide the headline and labels, then ask whether the reveal is an expected symbol that could stand for any brand. A legible arrow or checkmark is not necessarily an original idea; seek a consequence specific to the subject and its rules.
- **House-style convergence:** compare recent outputs side by side. If unrelated briefs repeatedly become the same headline/caption/dark boxed stage/control, sketch a different page structure and narrative rhythm before revising the central graphic. Change layout, pacing and art direction for a reason tied to the brief, not random decoration.
- **Light/shadow source break:** a cast shadow must derive from the same object's geometry, receiver plane and light vector as the lit facets. Detached decorative dark shapes are not evidence of a consistent physical scene; remove them or derive and inspect their projection through the motion states.
- **Decorative/content collision:** evaluate guides, rules, overlays and framing as geometry, not neutral decoration. At each viewport and state, inspect whether they run through glyphs, controls or focal shapes; move or remove them rather than relying on their thinness or muted color. A rule may intentionally structure space, but should not accidentally slice the promised subject.
- **Weak resolved state:** compare the initial and resulting frames without labels at the same viewport. A technically legible result that loses scale, contrast, placement or visual energy has not earned its reveal. Redesign the destination and the path together, including how they use the first viewport, before polishing the transition.
- **Reveal spoilers:** inspect title, helper copy, button, footer and accessibility text together. Provide enough instruction to operate the page without printing the visual answer before a discovery; put the precise answer in the appropriate completed state and keep accessible instructions honest. Hiding necessary information from assistive technology is not a fix.
- **Interaction-form convergence:** changing a dark stage to a light stage does not break a formula when unrelated studies remain a two-state showpiece with the same title, caption and toggle. Sketch a different user action or narrative rhythm, such as choosing, arranging or exploring, only when it serves the subject. Verify real agency, keyboard access, feedback and recovery; do not add fake interactivity for novelty.
- **Agency without an outcome space:** count reachable outputs, not just interactive controls. When free choices yield mostly incoherent combinations or dead ends, constrain the language or data model so each permitted path has a meaningful consequence; show why a choice belongs to the subject. Test several ordinary paths, long combinations, removal and dead ends, not only one curated happy path. If a constraint changes the promised freedom, say so.
- **Feedback away from action:** on desktop and narrow viewports, inspect both the control and its result at the actual moment of use. If the result is offscreen or detached, give an immediate local response that names the change, and provide a clear route to the fuller result without forcing a hunt. Keep that response accessible and consistent with the distant state; verify on a short mobile viewport and with keyboard focus.
- **Evidence-set coherence:** compare every cited rendered frame to the exact source revision and capture context it represents. A task-bound PNG is not automatically current-source proof: edits between screenshots, even a small alignment correction, invalidate a mixed before/after set. Re-capture the affected states from one revision, then verify the settled pixels and any host frame. Do not merge desktop and mobile proof from different revisions into one unqualified pass.
- **State coverage versus capture count:** six named capture slots may contain identical pixels, especially for a static page. Compare the capture digests and the visible behavior, label static-equivalent frames explicitly, and report which transitions were actually exercised, which were untested, and why duplicates are expected. Do not describe six citations as six distinct visual states. Do not fail a correct static or reduced-motion state solely because its pixels match another capture; the brief determines which transitions must differ.

Dogfood the REX MCP itself when building a study: freeze the brief in a REX task, inspect the `rex_design_workflow` prompt and original skill, use the scoped `rex_next`/read/edit/run/test path for changes, and submit evidence through its custody flow. Keep a miss ledger with the failure class, exact task/tool step, rendered counterexample, why the current guardrail did not catch it and the proposed general correction. Do not claim that merely reading the prompt or passing a test means an external agent obeyed it. Do not add a new scene-specific rule when an existing class can be tested more clearly.

In a landing-page Visual brief, identify one dominant hero object or product behavior and one plain-language message. In the first actual host viewport, measure whether the object is visible, legible and owns the composition on desktop and mobile; keep the primary action adjacent to it when interaction is the story. Remove decorative labels, redundant frames and explanatory furniture that compete with the object. Compare the hierarchy and crop to live examples for principles, not copied layouts or brand styling. Treat a tidy card and several small effects as insufficient when the brief calls for a strong visual idea.

Do not promote a claimed visual behavior from source code, an accessibility description, a slider value, or an in-flight transition frame to a fact in the handoff. Wait until the rendered state settles, then inspect the actual host-framed pixels at the claimed endpoint. If the copy says a line continues on the other side of an occluder, point to its visible continuation; if the screenshot only shows the near side, the claim is unverified or false even if the DOM width eventually changes. Capture matching crops at 0, midpoint, 100 and reverse, with the numeric state visible where possible. Separate observed pixels from inferred mechanics and name any mismatch before reporting completion.

Before approving a continuous Visual control, define a concrete pixel-delta proof. Capture the same camera and crop at its start, midpoint and end, then reverse to start. The changed object must move or transform enough to be obvious without reading the numeric label; a slider readout or SVG attribute change alone is insufficient. If a light, shadow, object, or scene state is the promise, confirm its visible silhouette and placement change at the correct place, while the unaffected anchors stay coherent. If start and end are almost indistinguishable at actual delivery size, reject the concept or enlarge the transformation rather than giving it a passing motion grade. Inspect the mobile host frame as well as desktop; keep the visual object and the control that changes it in the same opening viewport, including the host header. If the control falls below the fold, rebalance the page or attach it to the scene, then capture both widths again. This is a visual gate, not a source-text checklist or a guarantee of taste. Record the frozen brief, image pair, values, viewport, and remaining defects in the review.

Implement responsive compositions rather than shrinking the desktop scene. Make navigation and calls to action usable with focus and reduced motion. When the page is embedded inside a host or design system, verify the *rendered* link and control colors in that host, including visited state, rather than trusting local CSS: host rules can override a correct-looking source declaration. Scope the fix to the page and re-render the affected breakpoint; do not claim a CSS selector alone proves contrast.  Keep essential content visible without JavaScript or after an animation fails, and make the reduced-motion path immediate and readable. When typography carries the visual idea, test the page with the intended font blocked and with content lengthened or translated. Preserve the central composition without clipping meaning, navigation or calls to action. A font swap must not cover a control or shift a label away from its object; check first viewport and later content at narrow and wide widths. Use real words shaped like the target content, not filler; if no translation has been reviewed, mark it as a stress test rather than claim localization quality.  For finite or timed Visual motion, the reduced-motion equivalent should not flash a static end state briefly and then disappear on the animation timer. Let the state remain readable until a clearly labeled user action, or hold it long enough for the content to be understood; verify the settled state after the original timer window. For a script-dependent Visual control, do not leave an inert action visible when the script never starts: render a truthful static note and hide the control by default, then reveal it only after initialization succeeds. Inspect the JS-disabled first viewport with a fresh URL or disabled cache; a stale screenshot can conceal a failed fallback. Responsive CSS can accidentally override a script-off hidden control (for example, a mobile display rule after a generic hidden rule). At each breakpoint, check computed visibility and rendered pixels with scripting disabled, not just the desktop fallback.  Distinguish initial script failure from a later failed animation when the control was already activated. Render and inspect opening frame, later section, narrow/mobile crop, and actual animation playback at its start, middle, and end; for scroll choreography, scroll through it on desktop and mobile and check pinned height, overlap, focus, and exiting the scene. Separately render the reduced-motion path rather than inferring it from a CSS rule or source. Compare to references on observable traits such as image integration, type rhythm and composition; a screenshot does not prove its interactions or accessibility. Review whether it achieves its own visual thesis before claiming taste or superiority.

## Production mode: dependable pages

For Production briefs centered on a running task or agent, present the history of **one actual run** as a timeline: started, running, each observed event, result or failure, and any unknown outcome. Keep confirmed receipts inspectable and uncertainty visible in place. A set of selectable demo states is not a run and cannot prove continuity, recovery or outcome. Synthetic fixtures may test the UI, but label them and do not present them as real service evidence.

Identify who acts, the exact decision or task, data provenance, constraints and safe completion condition. Map entry, action, feedback and recovery before styling. Build a state coverage ledger; implement relevant loading, empty, error, invalid, success, permission and long/dense cases with truthful messages and preserved input. Avoid status/color contradictions. Keep the next safe action visible or reachable at narrow widths and 200% zoom. Use semantic controls, focus, keyboard, labels, contrast and touch targets, and test both pointer and keyboard paths. Visual decisions should reduce scanning and reveal the task's own shape, not add chrome for its own sake. Render real data-shaped cases at narrow and wide sizes and inspect the pixels and behavior. Never claim live data, verified permissions or production readiness from a local static demo.

## Brief and audit

Identify the audience, primary task, constraints, device sizes, and success measure. Mark assumptions when the brief is thin; ask if an assumption changes the product's purpose or audience. Inspect existing routes, components, tokens, assets, and conventions. For redesigns, capture the current UI and name concrete failures in hierarchy, navigation, readability, responsiveness, accessibility, or task completion. Preserve working content and flows. Never invent testimonials, metrics, logos, prices, or product claims.

## Interaction before styling

Map entry, decisions, primary action, outcomes, and recovery. Cover loading, empty, error, success, disabled, and long-content states where relevant. Dashboards need legible status, freshness, filters, and next actions. For async filters or refreshes, distinguish the requested view from the displayed snapshot while loading or after failure; label retained data as a previous snapshot and keep its counts paired with its rows. Ignore stale responses when a newer request has superseded them, then test a rapid filter change, failure, and actual retry. A mock timer is not proof of live freshness or service recovery. For a chart or metric, mark data provenance and denominator, distinguish missing from a recorded zero, and do not compute a continuous trend or adjacent-period change across an unrecorded gap. Keep exact values accessible in text or a table. For a table needing two-dimensional layout, confine horizontal scrolling to the table rather than the whole page; keep the heading, controls and text outside it reflowed, and allow individual cells to wrap. Label and focus the scroll region only when it actually overflows, show the way to hidden columns, and remove it from tab order when it does not scroll or has no data. At a 320 CSS-pixel viewport, inspect both edges and the empty state. A 320px viewport proxy is not an actual browser-zoom test. W3C Reflow guidance explains the table exception and its limit: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow . Do not color a rise or fall good/bad unless the product meaning establishes it. Tables need scannability and useful empty states. For priority or sequence editing, move stable item IDs rather than trusting similar display names or array positions. Provide keyboard-reachable move controls and announce the new position; disable impossible directions, preserve focus on the moved item, and invalidate an earlier review after every move. Keep a local order check distinct from a saved server order, and verify the exact ordered IDs, return/reset path and narrow layout. Dragging alone is not an accessible or sufficient route. For paged or cursor-based results, scope each cursor and in-flight request to its filter and snapshot. A filter change discards old pages and ignores late responses; a failed next page retains existing rows with their earlier-page label and offers a real retry of the same view. Do not present the number shown as an overall total when the service gives no total. Test filter changes while a request is pending, last page, zero results, failure and retry at narrow width. For a partial bulk result, show each item ID beside its confirmed outcome and next step; keep aggregate counts truthful, separate permission-blocked from transient failure and unknown outcome, and retry only confirmed eligible items rather than replaying a completed batch. For bulk review, bind the exact selected IDs and status labels to the displayed snapshot/version; a changed snapshot must clear or reconcile the selection and invalidate any earlier review before an action can proceed. Inspect the exact review, a data change between selection and review, and the focus path back to current records. A local demo without a backend must not offer a plausible bulk commit control. For a dense table, keep the item identity, status, owner, and next safe step together at narrow widths; choose a card/list transformation or an explicit scoped table scroll rather than forcing the entire page sideways. Test a long title and owner, several status types, filtered and unfiltered counts, and the route back from zero matches. If there is no save service, omit write actions rather than displaying a plausible but inert control. When reconciling disagreeing sources, do not merge a record by a single latest-timestamp rule. Show field-level values, source identity, revision and observation time, and keep a provisional choice separate from verified truth or a saved record. A source update invalidates selections and comparisons made against the old snapshot; re-read current evidence before any conditional write. A local comparison is not an audit trail or authority check. For destructive flows, identify the exact target ID, distinguishing details and current revision at review, not just a familiar display name. State what is deleted, who can be affected, and whether recovery is real, timed or unavailable. An in-page Undo that merely restores local state is not a service rollback. Exercise wrong-target input, cancellation/Escape, completion, the neighboring look-alike record and the return path; do not invent an active Delete control in a disconnected study. For approvals, quotes or other consequential decisions, bind a check to the exact amount, record and source revision. If the value or revision changes, invalidate the old check, show what changed and require fresh review. A green local check is not the person’s approval, a service acceptance or permission to commit money. Do not put a plausible Pay or Approve control on a disconnected example. For offline or queued work, separate editor draft, device-local queue, network dispatch, unknown outcome and service receipt. Do not label a local queue entry Sent or imply another person received it. Disclose storage durability across reload and device changes; keep the draft and operation identity while reconciling a lost response, and do not automatically replay a write on reconnect unless the service supplies conclusive status or enforced idempotency. Simulated reconnect cannot prove delivery. For a screen with sensitive record details, default to the least revealing view that still lets the person choose the right item. Reveal one field at a time with a keyboard-operable control whose name says which item and whether it will show or hide the value. Do not make masking a false security claim: the data may still be in page source, browser history, screenshots or network responses. On item switch, navigation, timeout or permission loss, hide values and invalidate any prior review; a copied value remains outside the page’s control. Test two similar records, reveal/hide, keyboard action, focus, narrow layout and stale review. A local mask is not a service authorization or data-leak prevention proof. For a multi-account action, show the selected sending or owning account at the point of review, not only in a global menu. Bind any local review to the exact account, destination and draft version. Switching account must invalidate that review, clear account-specific contacts and permissions, and preserve user-entered draft text separately until its new account and recipient are checked. A familiar recipient name in another account is not evidence of the same address or audience. Inspect a late lookup from the prior account, switch back, and verify which draft and recipient are active; a local preview is neither an authorization nor a sent item. For an import preview, keep raw source values, row numbers and parsed interpretations together. Flag ambiguous headers, duplicate identifiers, invalid units and partial rows before offering any commit. A recorded zero is data, not a missing value. Invalidate an earlier preview when source content changes and show both valid and rejected counts from the same current source. A local parser cannot claim service acceptance or live destination matching; without a backend, stop at review rather than a plausible Import button. For actual files use a format-aware parser rather than silently splitting quoted CSV on commas. When a workflow exports untrusted values to a spreadsheet, treat cell content as data, not trusted markup or formulas. Check formula-leading characters after parsing and serialization, including separators, quotes and control characters that can move a value to a new cell; a neat preview is not proof the downloaded file will remain safe when opened or re-saved. Verify the actual exported bytes and opening behavior in target spreadsheet software, and never call a local display-only check a secure export. See OWASP CSV Injection: https://owasp.org/www-community/attacks/CSV_Injection . Forms need clear requirements, errors, and input preservation. For collaborative edits, a successful local check on revision A is stale when revision B arrives: preserve the unsaved draft, show both versions and the base/current revisions, and block a plausible save or success state until the conflict is resolved against the latest revision. If a Copy newer text action replaces the editor contents, preserve a reversible route back to the previous draft and its base revision; a visible comparison that disappears after copying is not draft recovery. Distinguish in-page undo from durable persistence across reloads. Do not move focus out of an editor on every keystroke just to announce changed status. A preview or local check cannot replace a server-side conditional write. For optimistic state changes, distinguish requested or pending from service-confirmed values; a pending write must not wear a success badge. On explicit rejection, restore or reconcile the displayed item without losing the person’s input and explain the next step. If a response is lost, classify the outcome as unknown, not rejected, and check the operation before retry. Settings pages also need an honest distinction between current saved values, unsaved edits, a preview and a local check. For access or permission settings, compute the effective result from every applicable cap and explicit rule instead of presenting the edited role toggle alone; show which rule limits access. A draft check cannot establish live entitlement. Invalidate the check on any input change and never imply that an over-broad draft passed a permission test. For dependent controls, test each branch: hide or disable fields that no longer apply, clear irrelevant errors, retain values only when that helps recovery, and restore a sensible focus target. A passing local check must be invalidated when values change; if no save service exists, do not offer a plausible Save button. Put the task before an optional preview on narrow screens unless a task-grounded reason reverses that order. For a task interface that converts a local date and time to an instant, check time-zone rules rather than assuming a wall-clock label is unique. A fall-back hour can occur twice with different offsets; a spring-forward hour may not exist. Show the zone, offset and equivalent instant, block ambiguous or nonexistent entries before a consequential action, and seek the request author’s actual choice rather than auto-picking an offset. A local comparison does not authorize an event or notification. When a session expires during a draft or review, preserve the text and mark the check stale; never describe renewed access as a completed write or automatically replay an uncertain request. Give the person a safe route to copy or revisit the draft, and distinguish what survives an in-page return from reload/device recovery. A local mock cannot prove sign-in, access restoration, or service outcome. Test expiry after a check, editing while blocked, return with input intact and a fresh check; inspect narrow pixels. For branching multi-step work, reveal only the active branch, keep inactive values only as recoverable draft data, and clear irrelevant errors and checks when the branch changes. Bind a local review to the exact branch and values; back navigation may preserve input but an edited draft must invalidate the earlier check. Show whether the review is local, saved or delivered, and never leave a plausible Send action in a disconnected example. Test an invalid active field, change branches after review, switch back, and inspect the final current review at narrow width. Multi-step flows need progress and a way back.

Choose a visual direction tied to audience and product. Define a compact system for type, spacing, colors, surfaces, borders, and interaction states. Rank the primary action clearly. Use contrast, alignment, content order, and whitespace before decoration. Do not default to gradients, glass, oversized hero copy, generic AI imagery, or animation instead of hierarchy. Motion should explain state or direction and respect reduced-motion preferences. A reference library is evidence to compare, not a palette of required effects. Choose patterns for the brief and explain what you rejected; do not assume dark glass, bento grids, asymmetry, or hand-drawn textures are inherently better. Keep asset origin, license, responsive behavior, fallback, and performance cost with any adopted reference.

## Learn from examples without becoming one

Collect examples for the actual task, including relevant posts on X when a stable original and rendered result can be inspected, plus studio case studies, live sites, design systems, accessibility standards, and UX research. Record the URL and what was actually observed. A screenshot or promotional case study can show composition and intent; it does not prove conversion, accessibility, motion quality, or production behavior. If X posts cannot be verified, say so and use grounded alternatives instead of attributing an idea to X. Do not copy another skill's language, a site's distinctive graphics, layout, animation sequence, or brand tokens. Extract a mechanism and test it against our own content and user task.

For scroll-led Visual scenes, tie each scroll interval to a distinct content change, inspect actual trigger progress and rendered frames at start, midpoint, end and reverse, and check that pinning exits into the next content without overlap. Recalculate or recheck after responsive breakpoint changes; a desktop scroll proof is not mobile proof. Provide a static script-off and reduced-motion path. For Visual, make a short scene map before coding: each section's message, the object that changes, the reason it moves, the user's control, its final resting state, mobile pacing, and reduced-motion equivalent. Choose among scroll, page transitions, hover/focus, gesture, and state change. GSAP ScrollTrigger is fitting when scroll progress *is* the story; its pin/scrub/snap features are options, not a checklist. If the same reveal or pin could be swapped into any brand unchanged, revise the concept. Measure asset weight and check slow-load/failure fallback along with start, middle, end, and reduced-motion pixels. Non-essential scroll/parallax motion must be suppressible, not merely slowed down.

For Production, study examples for error prevention and recovery, not visual polish alone. Separate user input errors from app/service failures and incompatible or permission states. State requirements before entry; validate after enough input or on blur rather than marking unfinished typing wrong. Clear an error as soon as the corrected value is valid, preserve the rest of the form on failure, and provide a concrete recovery step. For a write that times out after dispatch, distinguish unknown outcome from confirmed failure: retain the draft and request identity, check the same operation before offering a retry, and do not issue a second write with a new identity merely because the response was lost. A weak eventually consistent "not found" is still unknown; retry needs a conclusive service status or a service-enforced idempotency contract with the same key. Local UI state alone proves neither. A retry label must correspond to a real retry path; if a mock always fails, say so and do not promise that retry will help. In a simulation, distinguish a local check from a saved or submitted record in both the action label and success copy. Exercise failure, retry, and the resulting state rather than inferring recovery from a visible button. After filtering to zero results, clear filters and confirm both the input and the rows reset; move focus to an actual recovery control when results disappear, and confirm keyboard activation restores the view. Test failure and retry with the existing filter state intact. If a form field name or ID overlaps a native method such as reset, check that the clear action still works. Keep visible totals distinct from filtered totals so the summary cannot mislead. Client-side validation aids usability but never replaces server validation; visual status changes alone do not prove that assistive technology announces them. Exercise the actual correction, failed submission, retained input, focus/announcement and return path before marking a flow done. Adapt any checkout research only to similar inputs and risks.

## Reference transfer test

For every outside reference, write down the user problem it solves, the mechanism worth borrowing, and the part that does not fit this product. Translate the mechanism into the product's own content, controls, and design tokens rather than copying its layout. Test the translated idea against the same task and edge state in the current UI. If it hides an action, needs invented content, weakens keyboard use, or breaks narrow screens, reject it even if the reference looks impressive. An adopted pattern needs a reason grounded in the task, not a similarity score.

## One product-specific interaction

Before choosing type or colors, consider whether a central behavior lets the person understand or change their task better than a clear static composition. If not, keep it static. For an interactive direction, name its visible object, governing rule, meaningful action and what happens when data is sparse, wrong or delayed. For a static direction, name the evidence and route. Make the chosen evidence or behavior clear from the first viewport without an explanation paragraph. A distinctive behavior earns its place only when it makes the task faster or safer; an unusual layout that hides a control is not an improvement.

Use external examples to challenge the bar, not to copy art direction. Compare only like-for-like evidence: same task and rendered states for a skill benchmark. If the reference has only a polished desktop image, judge visual presentation against that image, but do not infer its mobile, keyboard, error, or live-product behavior. Record where our build is weaker, where it is stronger, and which dimensions remain unknown. Fix the weakest actionable gap in the next build rather than adding decorative effects.

## State coverage ledger

Before coding, make a short ledger with one row per consequential state. Use these columns: user intent, entry point, data condition, visible feedback, next action, narrow-screen behavior, and proof. At minimum cover first use, normal use, no results, invalid input, failure, and a long or crowded case where they apply. Mark a state not applicable only with a reason. This is a planning aid, not a checklist that substitutes for examining the product.

For each row, check the words, data, and visual signal together. A passing fact cannot be styled as failed; a blocked completion cannot be labeled open or complete. If the primary control falls below the first viewport on a narrow screen, offer an honest visible route to it instead of hiding the recovery. At 200% browser zoom, check reflow, horizontal clipping, and reachable controls; a narrow screenshot or compositor page scale alone is not that test. If actual zoom cannot be exercised, use a half-width CSS-viewport proxy to find likely reflow defects, but label the true zoom result unverified.

For each row, ask whether the user can tell what happened and recover without losing their work. Prioritize a broken task or inaccessible control over decoration. If the task is only a visual critique, use the ledger to name missing states instead of pretending they were implemented. When handing off code, distinguish states actually exercised in the rendered UI from those inferred from source or left untested.

## Design decisions under pressure

Do not settle on the first attractive direction. For a consequential page or flow, sketch two meaningfully different ways to organize the task, not two color schemes. Compare them against the brief: Can a new user locate the primary action? Can a returning user see what changed? Can someone recover from a mistake? Which version handles dense data and a narrow screen without hiding essential controls? Choose one and record the tradeoff that made it win. If the brief does not support a confident choice, show the decision to the product owner rather than inventing a user preference.

Before final polish, run an adversarial pass on the chosen direction. Try a long name, sparse data, a permission error, a delayed response, keyboard-only navigation, zoomed text, and a narrow viewport. Keep the result tied to evidence: what was rendered and exercised, what failed, what changed, and what remains an assumption. Do not label a design superior to another product or skill without a shared task, comparable criteria, and actual results.

## Implement and verify

Reuse components and tokens where possible. Use semantic elements, real labels, keyboard access, visible focus, sufficient contrast, sensible tab order, and usable touch targets. Check narrow, medium, and wide layouts instead of shrinking desktop. Handle long labels, zero results, and realistic data density; preserve routing and state behavior.

Run relevant checks, then render the actual UI. Inspect pixels at narrow and wide widths. Exercise the primary path with keyboard and pointer, plus an error or empty state and long content. Fix issues and inspect the changed result. Source code, DOM, or a successful build alone cannot prove visual quality. Source-pattern and symmetry scores can catch known mistakes but do not measure task fit, accessibility, or visual quality; never use them as a pass certificate or optimize a page merely to raise a score. If rendering is unavailable, explicitly mark the visual work unverified.

## Evidence and review

Keep a small, reproducible review record for changed flows: viewport width and zoom; the starting route and data state; the action attempted; what the rendered UI showed; and whether the user could finish or recover. Capture the before and after for the same task, not only the best-looking final frame. If a visual choice trades away task speed, density, or clarity, name that cost and who bears it. Ask a reviewer to challenge the weakest state or assumption instead of voting on polish alone. No automated checker, screenshot, or single happy-path demo proves a design works for its audience. When using REX custody, compare the final page and review with the task and acceptance frozen at execute. If the central behavior changed, do not treat a file receipt or accepted submission as proof that the original design obligation was met; report the mismatch and seek an explicit revised task or narrower claim. Preserve the original task record rather than retroactively describing it as a different brief.

## Handoff

State the design direction and why it fits; changed files; paths and states tested; assumptions and remaining risks. Make critique specific: the issue, its effect on the task, and the smallest useful fix.
